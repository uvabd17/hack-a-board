This walkthrough takes the plan we landed on — CSV import shipping first, judging redesign behind it — and shows you what each screen looks like across organizer setup, the import wizard, judge UIs per format, the in-room display, and how the leaderboard behaves under each judging style. section 01The big picture

One organizer choice — the hackathon's phase timeline — drives everything downstream. Each phase has a judging style (or no judging at all, like a break or submission window). The judge UI, the in-room display, the participant screen, and the leaderboard math all derive from the active phase.

flow
organizer creates hackathon → picks default judging style + adds phases
    ↓
organizer imports CSV from Devfolio/Unstop → teams + participants exist with QRs
    ↓
event starts → display shows phase timeline + ticker
    ↓
phase activates → judge UI + participant view + leaderboard all adapt to the style
    ↓
phase closes → reveal animation, advance teams (if knockout), countdown to next phase
    ↓
ceremony → final leaderboard, winners

No numeric rank — that would be fake here. Tiles flip from PENDING → SAFE / MAYBE / CUT as judges vote. At window close, the advancement rule executes: teams marked YES by ≥M judges enter the next phase. Eliminated teams are archived but visible in the grid.

section 06Phase active: Panel deliberation

Judges meet privately, agree on placements, one captain submits the panel decision. No individual scores. The leaderboard cannot update during deliberation — instead, the display shows a "deliberating" state, then a dramatic ranked reveal when the captain submits.

section 07Between phases — the display isn't dead

why this works

Hackers don't want fake tension. They want to know when lunch is, who the mentor on duty is, and when the judge is coming to their table. The "live" feel comes from the operational ticker — actual useful info — not from inventing fake activity.

section 09Per-role view

Organizer

Owns the timeline

  • Creates hackathon, sets default style
  • Drags phases into the timeline
  • Imports teams from Devfolio/Unstop CSV
  • Opens/closes phases, sees coverage warnings
  • Triggers reveal at panel-phase close
  • Override + audit log for edge cases
Judge

UI adapts to style

  • Rubric: 1–5 scale × criteria → submit
  • Shortlist: YES / MAYBE / NO per team
  • Panel: captain drag-orders teams once
  • Window-gated: can't submit outside hours
  • Grace period if scanned before close
Participant

One QR, one screen

  • Magic-link QR from organizer (printed or mailed)
  • Sees own scores, round breakdown
  • Sees phase timeline + countdowns
  • "You advanced" / "Journey ends here" at cuts
  • Final wrapped card after ceremony
no spectator role

Tier 2/3 college hackathons don't have audiences. The display is for the room — participants + judges + organizers. No crowd voting, no public showcase mode, no broadcast surface. Build for in-room legibility, not livestream optics.

section 11What's NOT in scope

Explicit non-goals so we don't drift.

  • Audience / people's-choice voting — no audience at tier 2/3 college events.
  • Spectator/broadcast surfaces — the display is for the room, not for livestream.
  • Devfolio/Unstop API integration — CSV upload only; APIs are private-beta and not worth the dependency.
  • WhatsApp/SMS distribution — v1 distributes via printable QR + mail-merge CSV. WhatsApp via Gupshup/Wati is v1.2+.
  • Pairwise / Bradley-Terry judging (Gavel style) — researched, not used at this market.
  • Premium / paid tier features — we haven't decided monetization; don't pre-commit.
  • Live-ticker voting mode on the display — follows from no audience voting.

section 12Why these decisions — show the working

The plan above says what we'll build. This section says why — how the judging formats were chosen, what alternatives lost, and what we knowingly left out. Nothing here is a preference; every call traces to research or an explicit constraint.

how these decisions were made

A research agent surveyed real-world judging practice — Devfolio's judging docs, the MLH judging guide, Unstop's organizer material, the SIH process, Devpost. Two independent design reviews (codex + gemini) stress-tested the architecture from different angles. Where the earlier draft disagreed with the research, the research won.

The funnel — 7 mechanisms in the wild, filtered to what we build

We did not "pick three" judging formats. Seven distinct mechanisms exist in real hackathons. We filtered them against one question: does the tier 2/3 Indian college market actually use this?

Why shortlist is its own format — not just "knockout with scores"

An earlier draft modelled Round 1 as numeric scoring plus elimination. Research showed the real Round-1 mechanism at large hackathons is usually not numeric scoring at all. The deciding factor is the judge's cognitive task:

Rubric — evaluate

Judge has real time per team. Rates Innovation, Execution, Impact each 1–5. A multi-criteria form. Works at 20–60 teams.

Shortlist — triage

200 teams, limited judge-hours. The judge physically cannot carefully score each one. They make one fast call: good enough to advance — yes / maybe / no. One tap.

Different data shape (a decision, not a score) and a different UI (one tap, not a form). That's why it's a separate format, not a flag on rubric. Forcing 200 teams through a 5-criteria form produces rushed, meaningless numbers.

Why the captain method for panel deliberation

At finals, judges don't score independently. They hear all pitches, then deliberate verbally and reach consensus. The system only needs to record the outcome — and one person does that.

If all 5 judges submitted independently, the system would average their individual opinions — which is rubric scoring, and throws away the consensus the deliberation produced. The alternative (every judge ranks, system aggregates) is a different format family (pairwise / Borda) and doesn't match how college finals actually run. The captain is whichever judge the organizer designates as lead — chief judge, senior faculty, or a senior industry guest.

Alternatives considered — and why they lost

AlternativeForWhy it lost
Borda / Condorcet aggregation of independent judge rankingsPanelContradicts the verbal-consensus reality. If judges already agreed in the room, re-aggregating their individual ranks discards that. Math complexity for no gain.
Numeric score 1–10 with a top-N cutoffShortlistForces careful per-team scoring on 200 teams, which judges won't do. Rejected as the primary model — but kept as an advancement-rule variant ("advance top N by score") for organizers who want it.
One universal format with toggles, no distinct stylesEverythingThe on-screen judge task differs too much. A multi-criteria form and a single yes/no tap can't be the same UI without becoming a tangle of conditionals.
Organizer-defined custom scoring (a scoring DSL)EverythingToo much rope. A support nightmare. Nobody at the tier 2/3 college market wants to define their own scoring language.
Walking vs Stage as separate "formats"Format modelThey're a timing axis (continuous vs windowed), not separate formats. Modelled as an orthogonal toggle instead — avoids combinatorial blow-up ("Stage + Track + Knockout" has to be expressible).

section 13tl;dr

Ship CSV import first (2 weeks) so real organizers can dump their Devfolio/Unstop exports and stop re-typing teams. Then rebuild judging as a phase-timeline product where each phase picks its own style — rubric / shortlist / panel — and the leaderboard adapts: ranked list for rubric, Survival Grid for shortlist, War Room → reveal for panel. The display always shows an operational ticker during quiet windows. No audience, no fake liveness, no spectator mode.
